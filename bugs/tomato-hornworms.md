# Tomato & Tobacco Hornworms (Manduca spp.)

- **Type:** chewing
- **Houston timing:** June–September (peak late July)
- **Severity:** medium for the plant, but one worm eats a lot

## Identification

- Big green caterpillar, up to 10 cm, with a "horn" on the rear.
- Curled along a stem, they look just like a leaf.
- Adult = large sphinx/hawk moth, seen at dusk.

Two species, hard to tell apart:
- Tomato hornworm: V-shaped chevrons on the sides, red horn.
- Tobacco hornworm: 7 diagonal stripes, commoner on tomatoes in the South.

### Look-alikes
- Loopers: small, no horn, loop as they crawl.

## Host plants

Tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, potatoes.

## Signs of damage

1. Branches stripped of leaves overnight.
2. Large dark green droppings (frass) on the soil under the plant.

## Lifecycle in Houston

Overwinters as a pupa in the soil. Moths emerge in May–June and lay single eggs on leaf undersides. The caterpillar feeds about 4 weeks, growing fast each week. Two or more generations per year.

## Control (IPM)

### Monitor
- Look at the soil under plants for droppings.
- Find the worm: curled tight along a stem (Ref: UC IPM).

### Prevent
- Rotate crops and till/clean up after harvest to destroy overwintering pupae (Refs: UC IPM, UF).

### Physical
- Hand-pick and squish or snip them. In a home garden this is the main control (Refs: UC IPM, UF).

### Biological
- Parasitic wasps: if the worm is covered in small white cocoons, leave it — it's already dead (Refs: UC IPM, UF).

### Least-toxic products
- Bt (Bacillus thuringiensis) works best on small caterpillars. Spray in the evening (Ref: UF).

### Conventional (last resort)
- Not recommended by sources. Hand-picking and Bt handle it (Refs: UC IPM, UF).

## Field notes (personal)

- A flashlight hunt at midnight is real: worms shine against the leaves.
- Chickens freed in the fall cleanup turn pupae into protein.
