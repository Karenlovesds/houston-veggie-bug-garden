# Stink Bugs (Pentatomidae)

- **Type:** sucking
- **Houston timing:** year-round; worst on the fall tomato flush
- **Severity:** medium — they ruin fruit, not plants

## Identification

- Shield-shaped, ~1.5 cm, smelly when disturbed.
- Southern green stink bug: plain green.
- Brown stink bug: brown with a fine stripe on the antennae.
- Eggs: barrel-shaped clusters on leaf undersides.

### Look-alikes
- Predatory spined soldier bug has spiked "shoulders" — it's a helper, don't squish it.
- Squash bugs: squash/curcurbit specialists, longer and narrower.

## Host plants

Tomatoes, peppers, beans, corn, okra, citrus, peaches.

## Signs of damage

1. Dark pinprick marks on green fruit.
2. Sunken, corky, off-color spots on ripe fruit.
3. Deformed "catfaced" tomatoes.

## Lifecycle in Houston

Adults overwinter in leaf litter, active again by March. Up to 5 generations a year in Texas (Ref: TX A&M). Numbers snowball into the fall tomato flush.

## Control (IPM)

### Monitor
- Shake a white bucket or sheet under the plant weekly — they drop and are easy to count.

### Prevent
- Remove weeds and ground cover in early spring, before bugs build and move into the crop (Ref: UC IPM).

### Physical
- Hand-pick bugs and egg masses from small plants (Ref: UC IPM).

### Biological
- Egg parasites and predators help. Don't kill them with persistent sprays (Ref: UC IPM).

### Least-toxic products
- None recommended by sources for home gardens (Ref: UC IPM).

### Conventional (last resort)
- UC IPM generally does not recommend insecticides in gardens — by the time you see damage, the bugs have moved on (Ref: UC IPM).

## Field notes (personal)

- Corn planted next to tomatoes was a stink-bug magnet; keeping them apart cut the damage.
