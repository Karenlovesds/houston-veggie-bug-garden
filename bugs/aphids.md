# Aphids (Aphidoidea)

- **Type:** sucking
- **Houston timing:** year-round; worst in early spring and mid-fall
- **Severity:** low–medium

## Identification

- Tiny (1–3 mm), soft-bodied, pear-shaped. Colors: green, yellow, black, pink.
- Clumped on new growth and leaf undersides.

### Look-alikes
- Whiteflies: white, fly off in a cloud.
- Mites: too small to see clearly, no pear shape, leave webbing.

## Host plants

Almost everything: tomatoes, peppers, brassicas, beans, roses, citrus.

## Signs of damage

1. Curled, yellowing new leaves.
2. Sticky "honeydew" on leaves.
3. Black sooty mold on the honeydew.
4. Ants crawling up stems — they farm aphids.

## Lifecycle in Houston

Reproduce without mating. Many generations per year. Mild winters mean they survive all year. Numbers explode in March–April and October.

## Control (IPM)

### Monitor
- Check leaf undersides weekly.
- Ants on stems = first warning sign.

### Prevent
- Don't over-fertilize. Too much nitrogen = soft new growth that aphids love.
- Control weeds near the garden (Refs: TX A&M, UC IPM).

### Physical
- Blast with a strong water spray — knocks most off.
- Prune out curled, heavily-infested stems.

### Biological
- Parasitic wasps (look for "mummies" — dead brown aphids), lady beetles, lacewings.
- Don't use broad-spectrum sprays, or you kill these helpers (Refs: TX A&M, UC IPM).

### Least-toxic products
- Insecticidal soap or neem/horticultural oil. Cover leaf undersides.
- Repeat applications needed. Don't spray above 90°F (Refs: TX A&M, UC IPM).

### Conventional (last resort)
- Rarely needed in a home garden. Systemics (imidacloprid) only on woody ornamentals, never on blooming plants (Ref: UC IPM).

## Field notes (personal)

- One hard hose blast during an October spike cleared a large outbreak within a week.
- Nasturtium planted as a trap crop pulled aphids off the vegetables.
