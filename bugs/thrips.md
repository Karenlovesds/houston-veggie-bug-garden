# Thrips (Thysanoptera)

- **Type:** rasping/sucking
- **Houston timing:** spring spikes (March–May)
- **Severity:** medium–high on peppers/flowers

## Identification

- Tiny (1–2 mm) straw-colored slivers that hop when disturbed.
- Snap a leaf over white paper to see them.

### Look-alikes
- Spider mites: no hop, leave webbing.
- Aphids: soft-bodied, clustered, don't hop.

## Host plants

Peppers, tomatoes, onions, roses, citrus blooms.

## Signs of damage

1. Silvery-streaked, scratched-looking leaves.
2. Twisted, deformed new growth.
3. Peppers: corky streaks, dropped flowers.
4. TSWV virus: ring patterns on leaves, stunting. Remove those plants.

## Lifecycle in Houston

Fast: egg to adult in 12–16 days in spring heat. Mostly female, no mating needed. Peak March–May, then again on lush irrigated peppers.

## Control (IPM)

### Monitor
- Weekly tap test over white paper.
- Blue sticky cards catch more thrips than yellow (Ref: TX A&M).

### Prevent
- Control weeds in early spring — they're the nursery.
- Reflective silver mulch reduces landings.

### Physical
- Water blast to knock them off.
- Fine row cover over young peppers.

### Biological
- Minute pirate bugs and predatory mites eat thrips.
- Plant flowering alyssum, dill, cosmos near the beds (Refs: TX A&M, UC IPM).

### Least-toxic products
- Spinosad is the best option; spray in the evening.
- Most effective products need 2–3 sprays, 5–7 days apart.
- Rotate products — thrips build resistance fast (Refs: TX A&M, UC IPM).

### Conventional (last resort)
- Rotate modes of action (e.g., spinosad → abamectin). Never rely on sprays alone (Ref: UC IPM).

## Field notes (personal)

- A weedy strip left next to the peppers was the TSWV entry point. Cut it back 25 feet and losses dropped 80%.
