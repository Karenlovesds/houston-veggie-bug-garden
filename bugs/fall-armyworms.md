# Fall Armyworms (Spodoptera frugiperda)

- **Type:** chewing
- **Houston timing:** August–November
- **Severity:** high — can strip a lawn in days

## Identification

- Green/tan caterpillar with a dark inverted "Y" on the forehead, up to 4 cm.
- Drops and thrashes when disturbed (balling up).

### Look-alikes
- Cutworms: hide in soil, cut stems at ground level.
- Hornworms: big horn on the rear, no forehead "Y".

## Host plants

Lawn grasses (St. Augustine, bermuda), corn, vegetable greens.

## Signs of damage

1. Lawn looks freshly mowed at the wrong time — blades clipped to the dirt.
2. Dark droppings across damaged spots.
3. Green lawn at dusk, brown at dawn.

## Lifecycle in Houston

Moths migrate north from Mexico every summer on wind currents, reaching Houston around August. Females lay masses of eggs; one generation is 2–3 weeks. That's why outbreaks appear suddenly.

## Control (IPM)

### Monitor
- Walk the lawn at dusk; larvae are most visible early morning and evening.
- Feed damage starts as transparent blade tips that look like drought. Treat once large numbers appear (Ref: TX A&M).

### Prevent
- No preventive sprays recommended — rely on early detection (Ref: TX A&M).

### Physical
- Hose down patches at night to break them up and draw birds.

### Biological
- Birds (starlings) and braconid wasps eat them.

### Least-toxic products
- Bt is effective on caterpillars and safe for beneficials. Spray in the evening; reapply after rain.

### Conventional (last resort)
- Several labeled lawn insecticides work; apply per label as soon as heavy larval counts appear (Ref: TX A&M).

## Field notes (personal)

- August storm of 2023: green at 7 pm, bare by 7 am. One labeled evening spray stopped it; birds cleaned up in 2 days.
